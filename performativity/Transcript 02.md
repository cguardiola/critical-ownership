 The Greek term **ποιησις** (poiesis) literally means "making" or "creation." It comes from the verb ποιεῖν (poiein), "to make" or "to create."

In classical and philosophical contexts, poiesis has several related meanings:

1. **Poetry and creative production** — It refers to the craft of poetry and, more broadly, any artistic or creative work. The word "poet" (ποιητής, poietes) derives from the same root, literally meaning "maker."

2. **Production or making** — In a general sense, it denotes the act of bringing something into being, creating an object or work that has an end goal external to itself.

3. **Philosophical distinction** — In Aristotle's work, poiesis is contrasted with **praxis** (πρᾶξις, action). While poiesis aims at creating an external product (like a statue or poem), praxis is action performed for its own sake and intrinsic value (like living virtuously). Poiesis is also distinguished from **theoria** (θεωρία, contemplation or theoretical knowledge).

4. **Heidegger's interpretation** — The philosopher Martin Heidegger revived and reinterpreted poiesis in the 20th century, connecting it to the concept of *aletheia* (truth as "unconcealment"), and viewing it as a fundamental human mode of revealing and disclosing the world.

The concept remains influential in aesthetics, philosophy, and literary theory as a way of understanding creativity, artistic practice, and human making.
